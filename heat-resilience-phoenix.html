Google Research's Cool Roofs / Heat Resilience release maps building-level rooftop reflectivity for 50+ cities worldwide — including both Phoenix and Tempe. This page walks through the dataset and a proposed workflow for pairing it with a local heat-vulnerability index to prioritize cool-roof retrofits.

About the dataset

Beyond neighborhood averages

Free satellite-derived albedo estimates exist at roughly 10 m resolution — too coarse to tell one rooftop from the next. Google Research's approach fuses that 10 m Sentinel-2 data with 30 cm commercial imagery (Airbus Pléiades Neo) using machine learning and radiometric calibration, reconstructing a spectral reflectance profile for every urban pixel and collapsing it to a single albedo value per building centroid.

The method was validated against airborne hyperspectral measurements over Boulder, Colorado, reaching an RMSE of 0.04 against ground truth. Released June 2026 alongside a paper in Nature Communications, the expanded dataset now spans 50+ cities across 15 countries — up from a 14-city pilot in 2024 — built with the World Resources Institute.

For the Phoenix metro specifically, both Phoenix and Tempe are included as separate city extracts within the United States download, which is useful given how much the two differ in roofing stock, lot size, and canopy cover.

Suggested application

From rooftop pixels to a retrofit shortlist

A quick, defensible use of this dataset for the Phoenix–Tempe region: pair low-albedo rooftops with an independent heat-vulnerability layer so cool-roof outreach targets neighborhoods that are both hot and least able to cope with it.

01

Flag low-albedo rooftops

Filter building centroids within the Phoenix and Tempe extracts to albedo < 0.15 — the dark-roof / fresh-asphalt range Google reports — to get a candidate list of retrofit-worthy buildings.

Source: Cool Roofs dataset, US extract
02

Join to a census-tract heat vulnerability index

Spatially join the flagged centroids to Phoenix's published Heat Vulnerability Index — 358 City of Phoenix census tracts scored on AC prevalence, vegetation density, and socioeconomic variables from CAP LTER (Jenerette et al.).

Source: CAP LTER dataset knb-lter-cap.665
03

Rank tracts, cross-check against observed heat

Score each tract by (dark-roof density × HVI), then sanity-check the top-ranked tracts against a same-summer Landsat or ECOSTRESS land-surface-temperature composite to confirm the dark-roof clusters line up with real hot spots rather than data artifacts.

04

Export a prioritized building list

Output the top-ranked tracts with building IDs as a shortlist — a natural input for a cool-roof outreach pilot, a rebate program, or an environmental-justice framing in a city adaptation plan.

geopandas · phoenix_dark_roofs.pyPython 3
import geopandas as gpd

# 1 — load Phoenix/Tempe rooftop centroids from the US extract
roofs = gpd.read_file("phoenix_tempe_centroids.geojson")
dark_roofs = roofs[roofs["albedo"] < 0.15]

# 2 — load the CAP LTER Heat Vulnerability Index tracts
hvi_tracts = gpd.read_file("phoenix_hvi_tracts_2016.geojson")
joined = gpd.sjoin(dark_roofs, hvi_tracts, predicate="within")

# 3 — rank tracts by dark-roof density weighted by vulnerability
ranked = (joined.groupby("GEOID")
          .agg(n_dark_roofs=("albedo", "size"),
               mean_albedo=("albedo", "mean"),
               hvi=("HVI_score", "first"))
          .assign(priority=lambda d: d.n_dark_roofs * d.hvi)
          .sort_values("priority", ascending=False))

ranked.head(20).to_csv("phoenix_retrofit_priority_tracts.csv")

Field names above are illustrative — confirm the actual schema against the downloaded extract and the CAP LTER data package before running.
